Should the second confidence tier be switched on?

We deliver only people whose evidence is confirmed from both the job title and the department. A second tier holds people where the evidence points one way without being complete — a confirmed lecturer in a paediatrics department, for instance.

It is not evenly useful. It nearly doubles Healthcare, adds half again to Head of faculty, and adds almost nothing to Lecturer or Researcher. Mostly it adds a second label to people you already hold rather than new people: the unique total rises 6.6%.

Our view: switch it on for Healthcare, Pharmacist and Head of faculty only. And for Healthcare there is a precondition — see the leadership question below, the same “consultant” problem applies.

Is a “Director” at a university a head of faculty?

Asked originally as a question about 2,900 people. Reading the data showed it is much larger, and that it splits cleanly in two.

Never academic — 53,599 records. ceo 15,918, founder 6,952, managing director 5,568, general manager 3,513, owner 3,395.

Could be either — 172,611 records. director 144,252, president 11,625, vice president 6,324, principal 5,482. A university president really is its most senior leader, so excluding the word would lose real ones.

What narrows it: the organisation already does most of this work. A company scores zero for Head of faculty, so ceo produces 879 people of whom only 55 are confirmed, founder 31 of 411, owner 2 of 188. The real question is whether that residue should be excluded by name. Our view: exclude the first list, keep the second.

Should a past role still count?

Seven of the nine audiences count past employment: someone who was a researcher in 2010 and works in a bank today is still in the Researcher audience, on the reasoning that researcher is a profession you have rather than a post you hold. Head of faculty and Librarian are the exceptions and count current posts only.

If you want the others to behave the same way it is one line per audience — but we have not measured what it would cost, and we would measure before changing it.

Being straight about it

What we cannot claim

There is no accuracy percentage, and there never will be one

ORCID has no “profession” field. The job title is free text the researcher typed, so there is no correct answer anywhere to compare ours against. The previous keyword system is not a reference either — around 53,000 of the people it called healthcare professionals work in engineering, earth science, mathematics or the humanities. If we gave you “94% accurate” we would be inventing it.

What exists instead: for any person we can show the words they typed, which example word it matched, how close the match was, and whether a human or the model decided. Ten automated tests guard the specific ways this system has failed before.

Coverage is the ceiling now, not permission

Of 1.81 million ORCID profiles we can name a profession for 673,851. That leaves roughly 1.14 million people with no audience at all — mostly because none exists yet for students or for technical and support staff. CDP membership and opt-in together cost about 172,000 by comparison.

Reachable means opted in, not deliverable

Three consent bases are read and any one of them counts: marketing preference, advertising, and legitimate interest. The CDP holds no bounce or suppression state at all, so there is nothing for us to factor in. Treat the figure as the opted-in population.

Healthcare is the weakest audience and we know why

Clinical job titles are the least standardised vocabulary in the data: Resident, Registrar, Facharzt, Interno and Senior House Officer overlap in meaning and share no common word. Academic titles are nearly identical worldwide. That group has improved from 41% to 34% unsure over October, and it is the one we will keep working on.

We were wrong about which words would cause trouble, six times out of eight

Before each audience was read we predicted which example word would misbehave. audiologist, researcher and resident were all flagged as risky and all came back clean. pathologist, intern and pharmacien were not flagged and all three were problems.

The reason is useful rather than embarrassing. We were asking how ambiguous a word is in English. What decides is whether people in other professions actually type it — nobody outside medicine writes “resident” as their job, and everybody outside medicine writes “intern”. That can only be measured, which is why we no longer act on these judgements without measuring first.

The one thing that would change the most

ORCID also records each person’s degrees, and we have never used them. A degree is far more standard than a job title — MD, MBBS, BDS and PharmD mean one thing worldwide, where clinical job titles mean different things in every country.

That is exactly what would settle the 146,865 records where the job title is only dr or md. It is the largest single improvement available and it needs no new data — we already hold it.

One constraint we would design around: a degree is evidence of training, not of current work. Someone with an MD who now works in venture capital still has an MD. So it should raise confidence in a profession the employment already suggests, never assign one on its own.
