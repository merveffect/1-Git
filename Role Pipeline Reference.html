How the system works

The problem

ORCID is where researchers type their own employment history. Every field is free text: the job title, the organisation, the department. One person writes Professor, another writes Resident Physician, a third writes only Dr. There is no profession field anywhere. So the data existed and could not be used to build an audience.

The one idea

We never classify people. We classify the vocabulary, once. There are 310,299 different job titles in ORCID and 450,307 different department names. Those are what get classified, and the answers are stored. After that, giving 1.8 million people their profession is an ordinary database join.

This is why the AI cost does not grow with the audience. The thinking happens once over the words; the daily refresh is a normal query.

How a word gets classified

An AI model turns each job title into 768 numbers that stand for its meaning. Titles that mean similar things land near each other. We hand-write a small number of example words — called anchors — and place them in the same space. Then every real title is compared against all the anchors at once, and whichever group owns the nearest anchor wins.

The number that comes back is the similarity, from 0 to 1. An exact match is 1.00. Below 0.65 we refuse to guess and the title gets no group at all. In between is a real match carrying less confidence, and that confidence flows through to the person’s final score.

Three ways a title gets its group

In strict order of precedence, and the first one that applies wins:

SourceSet byWhat it means
humanseeds/role_title_overrides.csv A person read the evidence and decided. Always wins. 348 of these exist.
patternseeds/title_group_patterns.csv The title literally contains the word, so no similarity is involved. Used only for Librarian.
vectorthe embedding The nearest anchor group, scored by similarity. This is how almost everything is decided.

A fourth value, REJECTED, means the nearest anchor was below 0.65 and the title was left unassigned. That is a real outcome, not a failure: not every job title names a profession we recognise.

The two axes

A job title tells you the kind of work. A department tells you the subject. Those are different questions, so they are answered separately and combined afterwards.

This was measured rather than assumed. If you try to read the research field out of the job title, 73.5% of records tell you nothing — “professor” has no field. Read it from the department instead and only 1.6% are unusable.

The score

Each audience weighs three pieces of evidence. The weights differ per audience and are set in dbt_project.yml.

role_final_score = wrole × title_score + worg  × organisation_score + wdept × department_score title_score = 0.70 + (similarity − 0.65) / 0.35 × 0.30 (so 0.65 → 0.70, exact match → 1.00) = 1.00 forced, if a human or a pattern decided it org_score = from the ROR organisation type, per audience dept_score = same shape as title_score, on the field axis

Above the audience’s confirmed threshold the person is delivered. Between probable and confirmed they are held back. Only confirmed people go out today.

Three match modes

  • title_required — the title group must match. The field only adds score.
  • title_and_field — both must match. The narrowest and safest.
  • title_or_field — either is enough. The widest, used only for Healthcare professional.

3

What the quality numbers mean

There is no false-positive rate, and we will not invent one

A false-positive rate needs a correct answer to compare against. ORCID has no profession field, so there is nothing to compare to. The previous keyword system is not a reference either: around 53,000 of the people it called healthcare professionals work in engineering, earth science, mathematics or the humanities.

So every number below is one of three things, and they are not interchangeable:

NumberWhat it isWhat it is NOT
share below 0.75 How much of a group the model was unsure about. An error rate. See the two proofs below.
records corrected Titles a person read and judged wrong, with the records they carry. All the errors — only the part that was read.
coverage The share of a group’s records sitting in titles a person actually read. Confidence. It means looked at and not objected to.

Proof one: we removed 70,977 wrong records and the number got worse

On 21 October we moved junior researcher and its family out of the senior researcher group — records that were plainly in the wrong place. The share below 0.75 went from 0.1561 to 0.1620. The count of low-confidence records was 304,478 before and 304,478 after, identical, because everything removed had scored above 0.75. The denominator shrank and the numerator did not.

Proof two: the number is partly a measure of title length

The embedding averages over the whole string, so a long title can only ever sit so close to a two-word anchor.

head of national reference laboratory nrl for bovine tuberculosis → 0.711 librarian → 1.000

Both are correct. One is nine words and one is one word. So a group whose vocabulary is long and specific will always look dirtier than a group whose vocabulary is short, whatever its accuracy — and the number is not comparable between groups.

What we use instead

Every classification records how far it stretched from the example words and who decided it. For any person we can show the words they typed, the example word it matched, the distance, and whether a human or the model chose. Ten automated tests guard the specific ways this system has failed before. That is inspectability rather than a percentage, and it is what we have.

The one we chose not to fix

Researcher, established

Feeds the same two audiences as the early-career group

9 example words

senior researcher · senior scientist · research scientist · principal investigator · group leader · staff scientist · scientist · head of laboratory · scientific director

The split between early and established is made entirely by job title, and both groups feed the same audiences — so the distinction is carried and never used. Splitting Researcher into two audiences would be a configuration change and nothing else.

The largest single error in the project

junior researcher, 42,949 records, sat in the senior group for the project’s entire life. With junior scientific researcher and the rest that is 52,279 records in the group that means the opposite.

It reached senior researcher at 0.870 because “researcher” dominates the phrase and the embedding places junior and senior close together — two ends of one axis rather than opposites. A word that reverses a title barely moves it in the space. Every review query written before 21 October filtered similarity < 0.75, so none of them could see it.

And the same test caught us making the model’s mistake in reverse: we moved junior group leader out of this group, then withdrew it — a German Nachwuchsgruppenleiter runs their own group on their own funding and is an independent researcher.

5

The two groups that feed nothing

student (1,968,320 records, 11 anchors) and support_technical (1,458,782 records, 13 anchors) are classified and feed no audience. They are also where titles that belong nowhere else get parked, which is why their quality numbers move the wrong way as the review progresses — support_technical went from 34% to 37% unsure because we moved low-confidence noise into it.

That is deliberate and costs nothing today. It would matter immediately if a student or support-staff audience were ever opened, and at that point both groups need reading from scratch. support_technical is currently the dirtiest group in the project and that is a side effect of the review rather than a problem with it.

Raw layer

ModelWhat it does and why we need it
raw_orcid_researchersThe ORCID source, one row per person after deduplication. Why it exists: the source keeps roughly fourteen rows per profile — every saved version — and nothing in the column names says so. Before the dedup every downstream count was inflated and still looked plausible. It also applies the visibility = 'PUBLIC' filter once, here, so non-public data can never leak downstream. It carries the educations array, which nothing uses yet.
raw_audience_builder_bigThe CDP, one row per SNID. Reads three consent flags and aggregates with LOGICAL_AND, so every row for a person must consent. Why: the source has several rows per person and the conservative reading is the defensible one.
raw_ror_data_refreshThe ROR organisation registry, from another project. Gives us the organisation type — university, hospital, company — which is the whole second axis of the score.

ModelWhat it does and why we need it
int_employment_scoredJoins both dictionaries back to every employment record and produces the three sub-scores per (person, audience). Also picks the representative record: one row per person per audience, their most recent post of that type, so the evidence shown for Lecturer is their newest teaching post and for Researcher their newest research post.

Parent roll-ups are unioned here, and the label is recomputed after the union — a parent inherits a child’s score, so labelling before the union would be wrong. Queries you can run

Dataset prefix throughout: dat-analytics-eng-ec869189.dev_orcid_role_identifier_

Why is this person in this audience?

SELECT role_key, role_label, role_final_score,
       role_score, org_score, dept_score,
       evidence_title, evidence_org, evidence_dept, country_code
FROM   `...scoring.fct_researcher_roles`
WHERE  snid = <the snid>
ORDER BY role_final_score DESC;

Why did this title get its group?

SELECT title, frequency, title_group, decision_source,
       ROUND(similarity,3) AS similarity, runner_up, ROUND(margin,3) AS margin,
       matched_anchors, is_assigned, group_score
FROM   `...dictionary.dim_title_group`
WHERE  title = 'consultant';

What is each group carrying, and how unsure is it?

SELECT title_group,
       COUNT(*)                                  AS distinct_titles,
       COUNTIF(decision_source = 'PATTERN')      AS pattern_decided,
       SUM(frequency)                            AS records,
       ROUND(SUM(IF(similarity < 0.75, frequency, 0)) / SUM(frequency), 4)
                                                 AS share_below_075
FROM   `...dictionary.dim_title_group`
WHERE  is_assigned
GROUP BY title_group
ORDER BY records DESC;

This is the number section 3 warns about. Read it per group over time, never across groups.

Which anchor is carrying a group, and which one is leaking?

SELECT matched_anchors[OFFSET(0)]               AS nearest_anchor,
       COUNT(*)                                 AS distinct_titles,
       SUM(frequency)                           AS records,
       ROUND(SUM(IF(similarity < 0.75, frequency, 0)) / SUM(frequency), 4)
                                                AS share_below_075
FROM   `...dictionary.dim_title_group`
WHERE  is_assigned AND title_group = 'practitioner'
GROUP BY nearest_anchor
ORDER BY records DESC;

The diagnostic that found most of this document’s fixes. A high share on one anchor usually means a missing anchor — the titles arriving there are correct and merely unconfident, because the nearest word we gave the model was the closest thing available. 2,684 veterinarians matching dentist was found this way.

Every decision a person has made

SELECT title, title_group, decision, reviewer, reviewed_at, note
FROM   `...seeds.role_title_overrides`
ORDER BY reviewed_at DESC, title;

348 rows. Each note carries the measured reason, so a decision can be re-argued later rather than just re-made.

What would change the most next

ORCID records each person’s degrees, in an educations array that raw_orcid_researchers already reads and that nothing downstream consumes. It is the largest unused signal in the project.

A degree is a far more standardised vocabulary than a job title. MD, MBBS, BDS, PharmD and MLIS mean one thing worldwide — which is the exact opposite of clinical job titles, where Resident, Registrar, Facharzt and Interno share no common word. That is what would settle the 146,865 records where the job title is only dr or md and carries no occupational information at all.

Two things to design around. A degree is evidence of training, not of current work: someone with an MD who now works in venture capital still has an MD, so it should raise confidence in a profession the employment already suggests rather than assign one on its own. And the coverage is unmeasured — analyses/archive/04 asked exactly this question and has no recorded answer, so the first step is a measurement, not a design.

The dates in the same array are the other half. full_start_date is already read for recency, so career length — today minus the earliest employment start — is available without new data, and it is a seniority measure independent of what someone called their job. That is what the external role library’s career stages are built on.
